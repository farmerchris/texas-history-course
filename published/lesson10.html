Big question: Why did Texas secede, and what changed when the war ended?

Step 1 · Read

Texas leaves the Union

In Lesson 9, Texas grew fast, and by 1860 nearly a third of Texans were enslaved. Across the country, North and South argued over whether slavery could spread into new western lands. When Abraham Lincoln, who opposed the spread of slavery, was elected president in November 1860, Southern states began to leave the Union.

On February 1, 1861, a Texas convention voted 166 to 8 to secede. The next day it approved a document explaining why: the Declaration of Causes. Voters approved secession about three to one. Governor Sam Houston refused to swear loyalty to the Confederacy and was removed from office.

Read the source: what the convention said

Texas Declaration of Causes, February 2, 1861 · short excerpts

Texas “was received as a commonwealth holding, maintaining and protecting the institution known as negro slavery…”

The federal government “has for years almost entirely failed to protect the lives and property of the people of Texas” on the frontier.

Words about slavery and slaveholding appear about 18 times in this short document. The convention also listed complaints about frontier defense and Northern states, but it tied nearly all of them back to protecting slavery.

Think: The convention wrote this to explain itself. What does it tell you about its main reason?

The war in Texas

Tens of thousands of Texans served the Confederacy, most of them fighting far from home. Some Texans, including many German Texans in the Hill Country, opposed the Confederacy, and some paid with their lives. Texas was spared the huge battles fought in the East, but three fights on Texas soil matter.

GalvestonJan 1, 1863

Confederate forces under John B. Magruder retook the port of Galveston from Union troops, keeping it open for blockade runners.

Sabine PassSept 8, 1863

Lt. Dick Dowling and about 46 men in a small fort turned back a Union fleet trying to invade Texas from the Gulf.

Palmito RanchMay 1865

The last land battle of the war, near Brownsville. Confederates won, a month after Lee had already surrendered in Virginia.

Freedom comes to Texas

General Order No. 3, Galveston, June 19, 1865 · excerpt

“The people of Texas are informed that, in accordance with a proclamation from the Executive of the United States, all slaves are free. This involves an absolute equality of personal rights and rights of property between former masters and slaves…”

Union General Gordon Granger read this order in Galveston about two and a half years after the Emancipation Proclamation. Freed Texans celebrated June 19 every year as Juneteenth. Texas made it a state holiday in 1980, the first state to do so, and it became a national holiday in 2021.

Reconstruction: rebuilding, and a struggle over rights

1865–66
Presidential ReconstructionTexas wrote a new constitution and ended slavery, but passed "Black Codes" that limited what freed people could do.
1867–70
Congressional ReconstructionCongress put Texas under army control and required it to accept the 14th and 15th Amendments. The Freedmen's Bureau set up schools and helped with work contracts. Black men voted, and in 1870 the first African American legislators, such as Matthew Gaines and George T. Ruby, took office.
1870–74
Texas rejoins the UnionTexas was readmitted in 1870. Governor E. J. Davis built public schools and a state police force, and many white Texans resented his strong government.
1876
A new constitutionReconstruction ended. Texans wrote the Constitution of 1876, which limited the governor's power in reaction to the Davis years. You'll study it in Lesson 16.
What changed? About 182,000 people became free, but most freed families had no land and many ended up as sharecroppers. Violence against freed people was common. The economy was poor, and cotton would take years to recover. The rights won during Reconstruction were real, but many were later taken away by new laws. That struggle comes back in Lesson 14.
