Early Statehood & the U.S.–Mexican War

Big question: How did statehood change Texas's borders and population?

Step 1 · Read

A new state, a war, and a new shape

In Lesson 8, Texas joined the United States in 1845. Mexico had never accepted Texas independence, and the two countries disagreed about where Texas even ended. Within months, that argument became a war.

The border dispute

Mexico's claimNueces River

Texas had always ended at the Nueces River, which reaches the coast at Corpus Christi.

Texas and U.S. claimRio Grande

The border was the Rio Grande, all the way to its source in the Rocky Mountains. That claim even took in Santa Fe.

From war to today's border

1846
War beginsPresident Polk sent General Zachary Taylor's army to the Rio Grande. After a clash between U.S. and Mexican soldiers in the disputed land, Congress declared war. Early battles at Palo Alto and Resaca de la Palma were fought near today's Brownsville.
1847
U.S. captures Mexico CityTexas Rangers served as scouts. U.S. armies marched into central Mexico and took the capital.
1848
Treaty of Guadalupe HidalgoMexico accepted the Rio Grande as the Texas border and gave up a huge area, the Mexican Cession, which later became California, Nevada, Utah, and parts of four more states.
1850
Compromise of 1850Texas still claimed land as far as Santa Fe. In the Compromise of 1850, Texas gave up that claim in exchange for $10 million from the U.S., which it used to pay the old Republic's debts. This set the shape of Texas you know today.
Map task. Compare an 1845 map of Texas (a long strip reaching up into today's Colorado and Wyoming) with an 1850 map (today's shape, with the Panhandle). What land did Texas give up? Find period maps at the Portal to Texas History.

Who came, and where they settled

Statehood brought safety and cheap land, and people poured in. Texas grew from about 212,000 people in 1850 to about 604,000 in 1860. Where each group settled depended on why they came and on the land itself.

GermansHill Country

Fled hard times and political turmoil in Germany. A society of nobles organized settlement at New Braunfels (1845) and Fredericksburg (1846). In 1847 John O. Meusebach made a peace treaty with the Comanche that was kept.

CzechsCentral Texas

Came for farmland starting in the 1850s and settled the rolling prairies of Fayette, Austin, and Lavaca counties. Many farmed cotton and corn.

Mexican TexansSouth Texas

Already lived there. Tejano families ranched the brush country between the Nueces and the Rio Grande and in San Antonio. Many lost land to newcomers through lawsuits and pressure after the war.

Enslaved African AmericansEast Texas

Did not choose to come. Enslavers forced them to Texas to grow cotton on the rich soils and river bottoms of East Texas and the Brazos and Colorado valleys. By 1860, about 182,000 people, nearly a third of all Texans, were enslaved.

Geography decides. Cotton needs rain, warm weather, and rich soil, so plantations clustered in the wetter east. Ranching works on dry grassland and brush, so it spread south and west. Rivers carried crops to the coast. When you explain where a group lived, look for both the push that made them leave home and the pull of the land in Texas.
