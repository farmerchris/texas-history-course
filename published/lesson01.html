Step 1 · Read

Cutting a long story into chapters

People have lived in the land we call Texas for more than 13,000 years. That is far too much to study all at once. So historians do what authors do with a long book: they split it into chapters. In history, those chapters are called eras.

An era is a stretch of time with defining characteristics, things that were true for most of that period. During the Spanish Colonial era, for example, Spain ruled Texas and built missions, forts called presidios, and ranches. When Mexico won its independence in 1821, the rules changed, so a new era began.

Eras help us see change over time. When you can name the era, you can ask good questions: Who was in charge? How did people make a living? What changed from the era before?

Historians disagree about edges. Did the Civil Rights era start in 1945 or 1954? Different books draw the lines differently. The dates below are approximate. What matters most is the big change that separates one era from the next.

The historian's toolkit: two kinds of sources

Primary source

Made by someone who was there, at the time. Letters, diaries, photographs, laws, maps, and interviews with eyewitnesses are primary sources.

Example: William B. Travis's letter written inside the Alamo in 1836.

Secondary source

Made later by someone who studied the event but did not see it. Textbooks, encyclopedia articles, and most documentaries are secondary sources.

Example: an encyclopedia article about the Alamo written in 1990.

A quick test: Was the creator there when it happened? If yes, it is primary. If they learned about it afterward, it is secondary. Both are useful. Primary sources give you evidence; secondary sources help explain it.
