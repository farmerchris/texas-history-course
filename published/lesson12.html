The Age of Oil

Step 1 · Read

A storm, a gusher, and a new economy

In 1900, Texas made its living from farms and ranches: cotton, cattle, and lumber (Lesson 11). Within a few years, two events on the Gulf Coast began to change that. One was a disaster. The other was a fountain of oil.

The Galveston hurricane, 1900

Galveston was the richest city in Texas and its busiest port. On September 8, 1900, a hurricane pushed a wall of seawater over the low island. At least 6,000 people died, and some estimates are much higher. It is still the deadliest natural disaster in U.S. history.

Galveston rebuilt. It raised the level of the whole city and built a giant seawall. But shipping companies began to look inland for a safer port. Houston, about 50 miles from the Gulf, dug the Houston Ship Channel, which opened in 1914. Houston soon passed Galveston as the region's big city. Geography, and a storm, shifted where growth happened.

Spindletop, 1901

Near Beaumont, a hill called Spindletop rose above a salt dome. A local businessman, Pattillo Higgins, was sure oil lay underneath, but most geologists disagreed. An engineer named Anthony F. Lucas, an expert on salt domes, kept drilling. On January 10, 1901, mud and then oil shot more than 100 feet into the air. The Lucas gusher flowed an estimated 100,000 barrels a day, more than any well in the world at the time. It took nine days to cap it.

The boom

Peopleboomtown

Beaumont grew from about 10,000 people to about 50,000 within months. Workers, investors, and con artists poured in.

Companiesbig oil

Companies that started or grew at Spindletop include the Texas Company (later Texaco), Gulf Oil, and Humble (later part of Exxon).

Industryrefining

Refineries, pipelines, and the Houston Ship Channel turned the Gulf Coast into a center for oil and chemicals.

Energyfuel

Cheap oil fueled ships, trains, factories, and soon automobiles. Texas went from a farm state toward an industrial one.

The bust

Within two years, hundreds of wells crowded onto Spindletop. Each one drained the same field, so the pressure dropped. By 1904, the field produced only a tiny share of what it had. Many people who rushed in lost everything.

The boom-and-bust cycle. It happened at Spindletop, and again many times after.

It kept happening. New fields brought new booms: the huge East Texas field in 1930 and the Permian Basin in West Texas. Busts came too. In the early 1930s, so much oil flooded the market that prices fell to pennies a barrel, and the Texas Railroad Commission (Lesson 11) began limiting how much each well could pump. In the 1980s, a crash in oil prices cost Texas many thousands of jobs. Today, Texas spreads its risk across more industries, but oil and gas still matter.

People change the land

Geographers ask how people adapt to and change their environment. Galveston's seawall, the Ship Channel, and oil derricks covering Spindletop are all examples of people modifying the land to make a living. Some changes helped people, and some, like oil spills and drained fields, caused damage.
