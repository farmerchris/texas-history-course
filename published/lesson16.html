Step 1 · Read

A constitution written in 1876

In Lesson 10, Reconstruction ended and Texans reacted against Governor E. J. Davis's powerful state government. In 1876 they wrote a new constitution designed to keep government small and weak. Texas still lives under that constitution today, though voters have amended it more than 500 times.

Read the source: the Texas Bill of Rights

Texas Constitution, Article 1 · excerpts

Sec. 1: “Texas is a free and independent State, subject only to the Constitution of the United States…”

Sec. 2: “All political power is inherent in the people, and all free governments are founded on their authority, and instituted for their benefit.”

Article 1 lists rights such as freedom of worship, freedom of speech and the press, trial by jury, the right to bear arms, and equality under the law. Like the U.S. Bill of Rights, it protects people from their government. Unlike the U.S. version, it comes first in the constitution.

Principles you can find in it

Popular sovereigntyArt. 1, Sec. 2

Power comes from the people. Texans vote for many officials and must approve every constitutional amendment.

Limited governmentreaction to 1870s

The governor shares power with other separately elected officials (the lieutenant governor, attorney general, comptroller, and more). The Legislature meets only every other year, for 140 days.

Separation of powersthree branches

Legislative (150 House members, 31 senators), executive (governor and other officials), and judicial (elected judges, with two top courts).

Checks and balancesveto · override

The governor can veto bills; the Legislature can override with a two-thirds vote; courts can strike down laws.

Texas has 254 counties, more than any other state. A bond is borrowed money paid back with interest over many years, and voters usually must approve it. School districts are also local governments with elected boards; property taxes pay for much of your school.

Citizenship. Rights come with responsibilities: obeying laws, paying taxes, serving on a jury, and voting once you turn 18. Good citizens also stay informed, speak up at public meetings, and volunteer. You can attend a city council or commissioners court meeting right now; they are open to the public.
